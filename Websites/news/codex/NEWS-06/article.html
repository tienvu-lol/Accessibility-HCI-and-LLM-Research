The List That Put Eligible Voters Under Review

A county bought a matching tool. Its records could not tell two neighbors apart. Residents were left to untangle the questions.

by Mara Vale and Eli North

November 12, 2026 · Fictional investigation

At the kitchen table in her fictional Linden home, Lena Moss laid out a utility bill, a registration card and a handwritten note. All three showed the address where she had lived for years. A county list said her record needed another look.

The list came from a matching tool that compared names across public files. Similar names appeared beside one another, but the worksheet did not explain whether a match had been checked by a person. A suggestion had become a question that residents had to answer.

For this fictional investigation, our reporters reconstructed a small sample of records and interviewed invented residents and officials. The exercise illustrates a reporting method; it does not describe an actual election, vendor or county.

What a match leaves out

Moss was able to correct her record. Her neighbor, who worked nights, needed a second appointment. Both described the same problem: a notice told them what to bring, but not why their names had been selected.

A review can be useful when its limits are clear. A name match alone is not proof that two records describe the same person. Dates, addresses and the history of a record can change what an apparent match means.

The fictional county clerk said staff would add an explanation to future notices and preserve a record of each review. The proposed changes would let a resident ask a more specific question and understand the answer.
