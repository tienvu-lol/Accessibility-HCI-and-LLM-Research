Three Tailoring Obsessives Unpack a Designer’s Defining Decade

A pattern cutter, a collector and a costume historian revisit the sharpest shoulders of the nineties and explain why they are back.

By Imogen Calder-VossNov 8, 20269 min read

The studio smells of steam and chalk. On a long table lie three jackets cut from the same pattern in 1994, 2004 and last spring, and the people gathered around them cannot stop arguing about the shoulder.

“It is always about the shoulder,” says the pattern cutter, lifting a sleeve head to the light. “Move it half a centimeter and the whole body of the jacket changes character.” The collector nods. The costume historian, who has brought a notebook of sketches, says the silhouette was never really gone. It was waiting.

A good jacket does not announce itself. It simply makes everything you put underneath it look more decided.

That decisiveness is what brought tailoring back to the runway, the three agree. Designers who spent years on softness and stretch are now building structure again, with canvas, horsehair and a great deal of patience, and customers are responding by wearing their jackets over everything from slip dresses to old denim.

The collector has a simple test for whether a jacket is worth keeping: sit down, reach forward, and see if it still looks intentional. The historian prefers to ask who it was made for. The cutter just laughs and starts pinning a hem.
