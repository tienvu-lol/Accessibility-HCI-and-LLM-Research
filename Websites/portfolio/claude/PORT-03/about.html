The studio

One person, one workstation, and a render box in the hall cupboard that the neighbours can hear in February.

I am Juno Salvi. I model, light, animate and grade everything the studio ships, which is why there are only ever two projects in a quarter. Before this I spent six years in two larger studios doing the same work in narrower slices, and I do not miss the handovers.

How the work is made

Every piece starts on paper as a timing diagram: where the weight falls, where the eye should be, where the loop closes. A storyboard tells you what happens; a timing diagram tells you when, which is the only part that is hard.

The blockout round is grey and unlit on purpose. If a piece does not work in grey, no amount of brass is going to rescue it, and the longer a client waits to see something the more expensive it becomes to change it.

On motion and restraint

A loop that runs forever on someone's wall is not the same object as a clip someone chose to watch. The first has to be ignorable. That is why this site has a control in the header that stops every animation at once, and why the looping pieces stay still for anyone whose system asks for reduced motion. Work that insists on being watched is just a poster that will not sit down.

Tools

Blender and Redshift for most things, TouchDesigner when screens need to be driven live, DaVinci for grade, and a sketchbook that does more work than any of them. Hardware is one workstation and a render box; renders go out overnight and come back wrong often enough to keep the sketchbook relevant.

Teaching

One workshop a year at a Stockholm art school, on timing rather than software. Students bring a three-second idea and leave with a diagram; nobody opens a 3D application until the second day.
