Motion that asks permission

5 December 2025 8 min read Juno Salvi

Everything I make moves. That is the job. It is also exactly why every site this studio builds has a visible control that stops the motion, and why the looping pieces here stay still for anyone whose system asks for reduced motion.

Two different objects

A clip someone chose to open and a loop running on someone's wall are not the same object, even if the file is identical. The first has an implicit yes. The second has to earn its place in a room it was not invited into, and the way it earns it is by being ignorable.

What that means in practice

  • Nothing autoplays with sound. Nothing autoplays at all if the system preference says otherwise.
  • A single control, in the same place on every page, that stops every loop at once — not one pause button per tile.
  • The control states what it will do, and the page announces the change politely rather than silently.
  • Any information carried by motion is also carried by text. If a loop is decoration, it is marked as decoration and hidden from assistive technology.
“Work that insists on being watched is just a poster that will not sit down.”

The commercial argument, since there always has to be one

Half the places this work ends up — trade stands, foyers, waiting rooms, hospital corridors — have an audience that cannot leave. Motion that respects that is motion that gets installed. The last two installation commissions both asked, in the brief, how the piece behaves for people who find movement difficult. Five years ago neither would have.
