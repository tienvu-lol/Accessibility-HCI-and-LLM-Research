Performance budgets beat opinions

March 5, 2026 7 min read by Priya Raman performance · process

Three senior engineers spent two weeks arguing about whether a charting library was too heavy. The arguments were all good. The library was 140 KB gzipped, it replaced about 400 lines of our own code, and it would be used on two screens out of two hundred. Everyone was right, which is why nothing happened.

What ended it was not a better argument. It was a number in CI: a budget of 150 KB of JavaScript per route, failing the build above that. The chart route was already at 96 KB. The library did not fit. The discussion took four minutes.

What makes a budget work

  • It is written down before the argument, not during it.
  • It fails the build, not a dashboard nobody opens.
  • It is per route, because an average hides the one screen that matters.
  • It has a documented escape hatch: raise it deliberately, with a reason in the commit, and tell the team.

That last point is what keeps a budget alive. A budget with no legitimate way to change it gets quietly disabled within a quarter. Ours has been raised twice in two years, both times on purpose, both times with a one-paragraph justification in the repository.

“An unwritten budget is an intention. A written one is a decision you can be held to.”

The part nobody warns you about

Budgets mostly do not stop you adding things. They stop you adding things without noticing. Nine out of ten failures in our first year were accidental: a date library pulled in by one helper, an icon set imported whole for three icons, a polyfill for a browser we had dropped. None of those were decisions anyone would defend. The budget found them because CI does not get tired.
