Shipping an editor theme contrast-first

June 18, 2026 10 min read by Priya Raman accessibility · craft

I have made editor themes the normal way: pick a palette you like, apply it, squint, adjust. The result always has the same flaw. The comments are too dim, because dim comments look restful in a screenshot and are unreadable on a projector, on a cheap panel, or to anyone whose eyes are older than the designer's.

So with Umbra I inverted the process. Before choosing a single hue I listed every token pair the grammar can produce — comment on background, string on background, selected string on selection — and set a floor: 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for non-text. Then I picked colours inside what was left.

The measurement comes first

pairs = tokens x backgrounds       # 61 real combinations
floor = { text: 4.5, ui: 3.0 }
reject(pair) unless ratio(pair) >= floor[kind(pair)]

Sixty-one combinations sounds like a lot until you realise a theme has maybe twelve meaningful colours. Most of the work is bookkeeping, and bookkeeping is what scripts are for. The script is published with the theme, so anyone can re-run it and tell me I am wrong in specific terms.

The surprise

I expected the constraint to produce something ugly — high contrast themes have a reputation for looking like a fire alarm. The opposite happened. Being pushed off the washed-out greys I default to forced actual hue decisions, and the theme has more character than anything I made freehand. Three people have told me it is restful, which is the word I would have bet against.

“Constraints did not flatten the palette. They stopped me hiding behind grey.”

What I would tell theme authors

  • Comments are text. Dimming them is the most common failure and the easiest to fix.
  • Measure against the actual background behind the token, not the editor's average background.
  • Selection and diff states are where themes quietly fall apart; they are pairs too.
  • Publish the measurements. A theme that shows its numbers invites correction instead of argument.
