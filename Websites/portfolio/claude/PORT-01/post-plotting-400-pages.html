What I learned plotting four hundred pages

February 17, 2026 8 min read by Tobias Quill hardwaretype

Four hundred and six pages, if you count the ones I threw away, which you should, because the ones I threw away taught me everything in this post.

Pens are the consumable, patience is the budget

A fineliner is good for about thirty A3 plots before the line thins in a way you can see but cannot photograph. I now replace pens on a count rather than on a judgement, because judgement is unreliable at 4pm on a Friday. A spare pen in the drawer is the difference between a plot and a weekend.

  • Count plots per pen, not hours. Thirty is my number for this paper.
  • Paper curls with humidity, so tape two edges, not four, and let it move.
  • Dry runs with the pen up cost two minutes and have saved me nine sheets.
  • A plotter will cheerfully draw a perfect rectangle 2mm off the page, every time, if your origin is wrong. Check the origin.

Path order is the whole job

My first broadsheet took forty-six minutes. The same sheet now takes eleven, and nothing about the drawing changed — only the order of the strokes. Sorting paths to minimise pen travel, and grouping by direction so the carriage does not reverse needlessly, bought thirty-five minutes a week for the price of one afternoon’s work.

“Constraints make the type. Cutting the glyph set was the design decision.”

The typeface was a side effect

I cut a stencil face down to sixty-one glyphs because every extra glyph is pen travel. That constraint produced the only typeface I have ever designed that I still like, which suggests something uncomfortable about my taste when unconstrained. The toolchain is written up in the Paper Plotter case study.
